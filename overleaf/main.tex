\abstract{Beavers are returning to European rivers, yet we do not know how their dams change dissolved organic carbon (DOC), the carbon that fuels aquatic food webs and complicates drinking-water treatment. Earlier studies, mostly at single sites, disagreed on whether ponds add or remove DOC. To resolve this, we sampled water upstream and downstream of beaver ponds in 180 Swiss streams in winter and summer, and used causal Bayesian modelling, which tests cause-and-effect pathways while accounting for uncertainty, to ask when and why ponds change it. On average ponds changed DOC very little, but this hid opposing responses: in about three of four samplings DOC barely changed, while in the rest ponds added or removed it. Dams lengthened the time water stays in the pond, which favoured phytoplankton and thereby raised DOC, whereas most of the DOC arriving from upstream passed through unchanged. In summer, ponds added DOC equal to about a fifth of the seasonal DOC change that streams already receive from upstream; in winter they had no net effect. Beaver ponds therefore act as local control points rather than as uniform DOC sources or sinks. These findings help managers anticipate where beaver recolonization will alter DOC and water quality.}

\keywords{Castor, dam, freshwater, DOC, causal inference, primary producers, hydrology, water quality}

\section{Introduction}

Ecosystem engineers are organisms that modify the physical structure of their environment and thereby control the resources available to other organisms \cite{jones1994}. By restructuring habitat, hydrology and resource availability, they create localized control points where biogeochemical processing becomes disproportionately intense \cite{bernhardt2017,laudon2026}. Beavers (\emph{Castor fiber} and \emph{C. canadensis}), whose populations are recovering across Europe and North America through rewilding and restoration, are a widespread example: their dams profoundly alter river hydrology, ecosystems and biogeochemistry \cite{larsen2021,grudzinski2022}.

Beaver ponds trap fine sediment and particulate organic carbon, store carbon in sediments and floodplain soils, and raise water tables in ways that slow the breakdown of organic matter \cite{wohl2012,wohl2013,johnston2014,laurel2019}. Beavers also slow the water flow and open the tree canopy, which favours aquatic plants and algae \cite{johnston2017,nummi2018,ray2001}. These changes can raise the concentration of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) through the release of compounds by aquatic plants and algae, leaf-litter inputs, and the flooding and leaching of carbon-rich soils \cite{bedzki2010,ciuldiene2020,johnston2017}. They can equally lower it, because slow water gives microbes and sunlight time to break DOC down, and sediments and groundwater can trap it \cite{nummi2018,ledford2023}. DOC matters well beyond the carbon cycle: it fuels microbial respiration and aquatic food webs \cite{meyer1988,battin2023}, high concentrations can deplete oxygen \cite{stanley2012}, and DOC forms harmful disinfection byproducts during drinking-water treatment \cite{leonard2022}. Whether beaver engineering raises or lowers DOC is therefore a water-quality question as well as an ecological one.

Reviews report that beaver ponds increase DOC in most, but not all, cases, and that the response depends on vegetation, soil, hydrology and climate \cite{larsen2021,grudzinski2022}. Because most studies examined single sites \cite{bedzki2010,ciuldiene2020,ledford2023}, it remains unclear whether these inconsistent responses are noise around a common average effect, or whether opposing responses are themselves characteristic of beaver engineering. Resolving this requires a large-scale analysis that separates the beaver effect from the other drivers of stream DOC, such as the seasonal peak in summer \cite{mulholland1997}, land use and forest cover \cite{laudon2011,lemaire2022}, primary producers, and river--floodplain connectivity \cite{wilson2008,allan2021}.

Here we quantify how beaver engineering changes DOC in 180 Swiss streams sampled upstream and downstream of beaver ponds, using a national dataset and a causal modelling framework. We ask three questions: (1) \emph{Direction}: does beaver engineering consistently increase or decrease DOC downstream compared with upstream? (2) \emph{Drivers}: are beaver-induced changes in hydrology the main driver of DOC change, compared with changes in the primary-producer community? (3) \emph{Magnitude}: how large is the DOC change across a beaver-engineered stream relative to the seasonal DOC difference the stream already receives from upstream, and does it reinforce, dampen or reverse that difference? We expected beaver engineering to affect DOC indirectly, by lengthening water residence time and so favouring primary producers \cite{nummi2018,johnston2017}, and to do so more strongly in summer, when light and temperature are higher \cite{mulholland1997}. Because ponds can both produce and remove DOC, we expected the direction of change to differ among streams. The answers show where and when beaver recolonization is likely to change DOC and water quality.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study sites and sampling}

We sampled 180 beaver-dammed streams across Switzerland (Figure~\ref{fig:4}a). All were single-thread, low-order streams with one to several dams in sequence. In each, we collected water upstream of the first dam, where the flow was not yet dammed, and downstream of the last dam, beyond the influence of the ponds. At 158 streams we sampled once in winter (December to February 2021/2022) and once in summer (June or July 2022); the other 22 streams were sampled once in summer, and at 16 of these we also measured primary producers. A few streams contained more than one separately sampled dam sequence. The 180 upstream--downstream pairs are the units of analysis; the 158 streams with both seasons are used for within-stream seasonal comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:4}). Further site details are in Appendix S1 (Supplementary Methods).

\subsection{Variables}

We measured DOC in filtered (0.22~\(\mu\)m) samples by high-temperature combustion and infrared detection (analytical error 0.2~mg~C~L\(^{-1}\)), and calculated the net DOC change as \(\Delta DOC\) = downstream minus upstream concentration. We described beaver engineering by the number of dams and the maximum dam height. We calculated water residence time as pond volume, derived from aerial imagery and a digital elevation model (DEM), divided by stream discharge from the PREVAH hydrological model \cite{viviroli2009}. We also derived channel gradient from the DEM and solar radiation from a digital surface model \cite{swisstopo2022,foen2021,muenchow2017}. At the 16 characterized ponds we measured the primary producers that can add DOC: phytoplankton (dark-field imaging microscopy of 50~L water samples), macrophytes (plant counts), and litter cover (dead leaves and twigs on the soil next to the pond). We delineated catchment and floodplain areas from the DEM \cite{lindsay2016,clubb2017}. Full descriptions and equations are in Appendix S1.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

We fitted all models in a Bayesian framework and propagated the 0.2~mg~C~L\(^{-1}\) measurement error of each DOC value through them. We report posterior means or medians with 95\% Bayesian credible intervals (BCI), and we considered an effect supported when more than 90\% of its posterior distribution lay on one side of zero.

\emph{Direction (Q1).} We modelled upstream and downstream concentrations, season and their interaction, with random intercepts for stream and stream-by-season, and a heavy-tailed Student-\(t\) error to accommodate the long tails of DOC data (Equation~\ref{eq:direction}).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{DOC}_i &\sim \text{Student-}t(\nu,\ \mu_i,\ \sigma) \\
\mu_i &= \alpha_{\text{season},\text{location}} + u_{\text{stream}} + u_{\text{stream}\times\text{season}} \\
\Delta DOC_s &= \alpha_{s,\text{down}} - \alpha_{s,\text{up}}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:direction}
\end{equation}
We considered a change larger than \(\pm0.5\)~mg~L\(^{-1}\) as ecologically relevant, because changes of this size begin to affect light, nutrients and the balance between autotrophic and heterotrophic processes \cite{thrane2014,seekell2015}. Values within \(\pm0.5\)~mg~L\(^{-1}\) form the region of practical equivalence (ROPE) \cite{pan2025}, which we applied to the seasonal mean \(\Delta DOC\) and to the predicted \(\Delta DOC\) of a single sampling (sink, negligible or source response).

\emph{Drivers (Q2).} We described the hypothesized causal pathways in a directed acyclic graph (DAG), a diagram of assumed cause-and-effect links, and fitted it as a structural causal model, i.e. a set of linked Bayesian regressions \cite{arif2023,gelman2013} (Figure~\ref{fig:2}; hypotheses in Appendix S1: Table S1). We used downstream DOC as the outcome, with upstream DOC, primary producers (phytoplankton, macrophytes, litter cover) and water residence time as its predictors, and dam number, dam height, channel gradient and solar radiation acting on them. This parameterization is equivalent to modelling \(\Delta DOC\) directly. Alternative causal structures, prior sensitivity, unmeasured-confounding and spatial checks are in Appendix S1.

\emph{Magnitude (Q3).} For each stream we defined the background seasonal contrast \(S\) (the winter-to-summer change in upstream DOC), the DOC change in each season \(\Delta DOC_s\), the relative response \(R_s\), the beaver modification of the seasonal contrast \(B\), the seasonal gain \(G\), and the footprint contrast \(F\) (Equation~\ref{eq:derived}).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S &= DOC_{up}^{summer} - DOC_{up}^{winter}, &
\Delta DOC_s &= DOC_{down,s} - DOC_{up,s} \\
R_s &= \Delta DOC_s / |S|, &
B &= \Delta DOC_{summer} - \Delta DOC_{winter} \\
G &= B / S, &
F &= A_{floodplain} / A_{beaver}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:derived}
\end{equation}
\(R_s > 0\) means the pond adds DOC. \(G > 0\) means beaver engineering reinforces the background seasonal contrast, \(-1 < G < 0\) dampens it, and \(G < -1\) reverses it. To avoid unstable ratios when \(S\) is small, we computed all quantities from the posterior of a hierarchical measurement-error model of the four concentrations per stream, classified streams when the posterior probability of a class exceeded 90\%, and obtained population shares by classifying every stream on every posterior draw (Appendix S1). We ran all analyses in R with \emph{brms}; code and data are available on GitHub (see Data and code availability).

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Beaver ponds add DOC in some streams and remove it in others}

\emph{We found} that beaver engineering did not change DOC in a consistent direction. The average \(\Delta DOC\) was 0.14 mg/L in summer (95\% BCI: 0.05 ; 0.22 mg/L; P(\(\Delta DOC > 0\)) \textgreater{} 0.99) and 0.00 mg/L in winter (95\% BCI: -0.06 ; 0.07 mg/L), both within the ROPE of \(\pm 0.5\) mg/L. Individual responses were variable and heavy-tailed (Figure~\ref{fig:1}): for a single sampling of a beaver-engineered stream, the probability of a negligible \(\Delta DOC\) was 75\% in summer and 77\% in winter, of a DOC source response 16\% in summer and 12\% in winter, and of a DOC sink response 9\% in summer and 12\% in winter (95\% prediction intervals: -1.9 ; 2.3 mg/L in summer, -2.1 ; 2.1 mg/L in winter). This spread is an order of magnitude wider than the analytical error (0.2 mg/L), so it is not measurement noise. We expected ponds to raise DOC in most streams, as earlier reviews suggest \cite{larsen2021,grudzinski2022}; instead, ponds added and removed DOC about equally often, with a slight excess of gains in summer. A near-zero average therefore hides the size of the changes, and it reconciles earlier site-specific studies that reported ponds as either DOC sources or sinks \cite{larsen2021,grudzinski2022}. We did not sample reaches without dams, so we cannot say how much of the spread would also occur in undammed streams.

\subsection{Hydrology raises DOC indirectly, through primary producers}

\emph{We found} that downstream DOC was set mainly by the DOC arriving from upstream: each additional 1 mg/L entering a pond left it as 0.94 mg/L (95\% BCI: 0.89 ; 0.99), so ponds largely pass on the DOC they receive (Figure~\ref{fig:2}). Beaver engineering added to this background through the primary producers and the litter on the adjacent soil: phytoplankton (standardized effect +0.14), macrophytes (+0.09) and litter cover (+0.07) all increased downstream DOC. Solar radiation increased both macrophytes (+0.15) and phytoplankton (+0.18). Longer water residence time further increased phytoplankton (+0.11); it was longer with more dams (+0.07) and shorter on steeper channels (-0.08). We found no evidence that dam height affected residence time, or that residence time acted on DOC directly. We had expected hydrology to be the main driver of DOC change; the data indicate instead that hydrology matters because it changes the producers that release DOC, which fits earlier observations that slow, lit water favours plants and algae and raises DOC in dammed streams \cite{bedzki2010,johnston2017,nummi2018}. These effects rest on the 16 characterized ponds and on literature-based priors, but they were robust to alternative causal structures and to halving the prior width (Appendix S1).

\subsection{Ponds redistribute the seasonal DOC contrast streams receive from upstream}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/media/figure3_v2.png}
\caption{a) Posterior distribution of the median relative response \(R_s = \Delta DOC_s / |S|\) across the 158 streams, in winter and summer; point: posterior median, bars: 66\% and 95\% credible intervals; dashed line: no response; skyblue: beaver-engineering adds DOC (posterior probability \textgreater{} 90\%), grey: no-evidence. b) Relative response of each stream in summer: posterior median with 66\% (thick) and 95\% (thin) credible intervals, streams ranked by their median; the grey band marks \(|R_{summer}| < 0.1\); the vertical axis is linear between -0.1 and 0.1 and logarithmic beyond. \end{figure}

\emph{We found} that, in summer, beaver engineering added DOC equal to a median 21\% of the background seasonal contrast \(S\) across the 158 streams (95\% BCI: 11 ; 33\%), and had no net effect in winter (Figure~\ref{fig:3}a). The average hid large differences among streams (Figure~\ref{fig:3}b): in summer, 39\% of streams (62 of 158) were clear DOC sources and 17\% (27 of 158) were clear DOC sinks. The seasonal gain was equally mixed (Figure~\ref{fig:3}c): ponds reinforced the background seasonal contrast in 43\% of streams (95\% BCI: 37 ; 49\%), dampened it in 37\% (31 ; 42\%) and reversed it in 21\% (16 ; 25\%). The ponded area was a median 0.4\% of the floodplain area over which the background seasonal contrast arises (footprint contrast F, median 256). We expected stronger effects in summer, when light and warmth favour primary producers, and this is what we found; the winter result is consistent with a smaller biological contribution in the cold season \cite{mulholland1997,battin2023}. What was not known before is that ponds do not push the seasonal DOC cycle one way: each stream reinforces, dampens or reverses it on its own terms, and the pond effect is a sizable fraction of the seasonal signal despite the small area ponds occupy. Beaver ponds thus behave as local control points, not as uniform DOC sources or sinks.

\subsection{Implications for water quality and management}

Because the direction of change differs among streams, beaver recolonization should not be expected to raise or lower DOC everywhere; where ponds add DOC in summer, they could add to the DOC load that drinking-water treatment must handle \cite{leonard2022}, and could shift food webs towards microbial pathways \cite{stanley2012,xenopoulos2017}. Our results describe DOC concentrations at two seasons, not discharge-weighted fluxes, so they do not show whether ponds are net carbon sources or sinks for the river network; vertical and lateral exchange with the floodplain, which can also affect DOC \cite{larsen2021}, were not measured; and the producer pathways come from 16 ponds. Within these limits, our results show that the context in which a pond sits, its hydrology and its primary producers, rather than the presence of a dam alone, determines whether it adds or removes DOC, which gives managers a basis for anticipating where and when beaver recolonization will affect water quality.
